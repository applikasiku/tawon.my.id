# The digital security checklist worth doing this weekend

A calmer, more practical way to protect your email, passwords and online accounts—starting with the settings that matter most.

A useful security routine does not begin with buying another app. It begins with understanding which accounts you would struggle to lose. Your main email, the number that receives verification messages, and the accounts you use for work deserve attention before an old shopping login. This guide turns that review into a manageable weekend project. The order and examples below are an editorial workflow, rather than a promise that any checklist can eliminate fraud.

## Start with a map of your essential accounts

Write down the services you depend on, using account names rather than passwords. Include your main email, phone provider, cloud storage, social accounts and any service that handles customer orders. Beside each one, note the recovery email or number you expect it to use. You are looking for dependencies: if the same email resets every other password, that mailbox is a particularly important starting point.

For example, a small shop owner might discover that their marketplace, delivery service and photo archive all depend on an email account created years ago. Reviewing that mailbox first is more useful than securing ten low-priority accounts at random. Keep your inventory private. It should help you navigate settings, not become another place where sensitive credentials are stored.

## Replace reused passwords in a sensible order

The core principle is straightforward: different accounts should not share the same password. CISA's Secure Our World materials recommend strong, unique passwords and a password manager. A manager can help generate and store credentials, but it also becomes an important account to protect. Understand its recovery process before relying on it for everything.

Begin with the accounts from your dependency map. As you change each password, confirm that the new credential has been saved correctly and that you can still sign in. Avoid changing every account at once if you cannot keep track of the process. A small completed batch is preferable to a rushed reset that leaves you uncertain which password belongs where. Never send a full password list through a group chat or an ordinary shared document.

## Add another layer of authentication

Multifactor authentication adds another check beyond a password. The options differ between services, and availability can change. Open the security settings of the actual service rather than following an unsolicited setup link. Where offered, review passkeys or security keys as well as authenticator options. Read the provider's explanation of how a new device is added and how access is recovered.

Do not finish setup until you have considered what happens if your phone is lost. If backup codes are provided, store them somewhere protected and accessible to you without that same phone. An example is a secure offline record kept separately from the device. Test your understanding of the process without deliberately locking yourself out. A recovery arrangement you understand is more valuable than a setting you enabled without knowing its consequences.

## Inspect sessions and connected applications

Many services show devices that are currently signed in. Review that list and investigate entries you do not recognize. Location labels can be approximate, so a different city is not by itself proof of intrusion. Compare the device, browser and time with your own activity. Use the provider's official guidance if something remains unexplained.

Connected applications deserve a separate look. A scheduling app, browser extension or abandoned integration may retain access long after you stop using it. Remove permissions you no longer need, after checking whether a current workflow depends on them. If you run a business, record what you disconnect so a colleague is not surprised when a tool stops working. Keep the review focused on permissions, rather than assuming that every unfamiliar technical label is dangerous.

## Make suspicious requests easier to check

Security often becomes difficult when a message creates urgency. Instead of deciding inside the message, step outside it. If an email claims there is a problem with an account, open the service through your usual app or a bookmark you already trust. If a colleague requests an unusual transfer or confidential file, verify through an established channel.

Consider this example: a message appears to come from a delivery company and asks you to install an attachment to correct an address. You already have the delivery app. Check the order there, or contact support through its official help section. The important habit is independent verification. Polished branding, fluent language and a familiar profile photo are not enough to establish that a request is legitimate.

## Give updates and backups a place in the routine

CISA includes software updates among its basic security habits. Review operating system, browser and application updates through their built-in controls. Decide when your devices can restart without interrupting important work. If a device no longer receives security updates, include that fact in your longer-term replacement plan rather than assuming that installing another utility solves the issue.

For files, distinguish an account login from a recoverable copy. A photo collection synchronized everywhere may still be vulnerable to an accidental deletion that also synchronizes. Ask what versions exist and whether a separate backup can be restored. Pick a harmless sample file and check the recovery instructions. This is a practical confidence check; it does not require deleting your main files or disrupting a working computer.

## A short checklist for a shared household or small team

- Identify the email and phone number that control your most important accounts.
- Replace reused passwords and verify that the new credentials are stored correctly.
- Enable an additional authentication method where available and plan recovery.
- Review signed-in devices and remove unused application permissions.
- Establish a separate way to verify unexpected requests.
- Check updates and confirm that important files have a recoverable backup.

If several people share responsibility, agree who owns each account and who may approve changes. Avoid making one person the only holder of every recovery method without a documented continuity plan. The appropriate arrangement depends on the service and your organization's rules. This checklist is a starting point for discussion, not a substitute for professional incident response when a business account is already compromised.

## Next steps: make the review repeatable

Choose one recurring moment to revisit the inventory, such as the first weekend of a new season or after replacing a phone. Record the date you completed a review and the tasks still outstanding. You do not need a complicated score. Being able to explain your recovery process and recognize your active devices is already a useful outcome.

If you believe an account has been accessed without permission, follow the provider's official compromised-account process promptly. This article covers prevention, not every emergency scenario.
