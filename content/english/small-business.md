# The hidden costs to check before selling a product online

A practical worksheet for seeing what remains after materials, packaging, platform fees and the work that happens between an order and a delivery.

A selling price can look attractive until the small costs around each order are counted. Packaging is bought in batches, returns happen later, and time spent answering messages rarely appears on a purchase receipt. This guide offers an original costing worksheet for a small product business. It is educational, uses hypothetical figures and does not determine your local tax obligations, accounting treatment or the right price for your market.

## Start with a clearly defined unit

Decide what one unit means before entering costs. Is it one notebook, a set of three prints, or a bundle with a gift box? If the definition changes halfway through the sheet, the result will be difficult to interpret. Write down the contents of the unit and the condition in which it is ready to ship.

For example, a handmade stationery set might include printed sheets, a cover, a binding ring and protective packaging. A refill could contain fewer components but still require handling and an envelope. Treat those as separate products until you understand their costs. This does not require expensive software. A clearly labeled sheet with a date, a unit definition and a source for each estimate is a useful starting point.

## Separate direct materials from production losses

List the materials consumed by a sellable unit. Use usable output rather than the number you hoped a batch would produce. If a batch contains damaged sheets, test pieces or unavoidable offcuts, those materials still cost money. Recording the actual result helps prevent a neat theoretical figure from disguising a recurring loss.

Consider a hypothetical batch using 120 sheets to make ten complete sets. The material use is twelve sheets per finished set, even if the design contains only eleven. The extra sheet equivalent might reflect testing or spoilage. This example is not an industry benchmark. Its purpose is to show why a finished-product count matters. Repeat the measurement on more than one batch before assuming an unusually good or bad day is representative.

## Count the work around production

Making the item is only part of fulfilling an order. Other tasks can include checking quality, picking components, printing a shipping label and protecting the parcel. Write down these stages before deciding how to represent labor. A business with employees, a solo maker and a partner workshop may use different arrangements.

If you choose to estimate time per unit, label it as an estimate and record how it was measured. A sample based on five uncomplicated orders may not describe a personalized product that needs several conversations. Keep exceptional work visible. You may discover that one variation looks profitable only because its extra preparation has been averaged into simpler orders. That finding is useful for redesigning the product, even before you change its price.

## Read fees from the channel you actually use

Platform fees are not interchangeable. They can depend on location, category, seller program, promotion and the date of the transaction. Look at the current agreement and a real settlement statement rather than copying a percentage from another seller. Separate the customer-facing price from the amount the channel pays out.

Build a sample order using clearly labeled hypothetical numbers. Suppose a product sells for 30 currency units and a channel deducts 6 in combined transaction costs for that particular example. The remainder before your other costs is 24, not 30. If a seller-funded discount or shipping contribution is separate, show it separately. Do not assume the illustrative deduction applies to any actual marketplace. Reconcile the sheet with your own statements before using it for decisions.

## Keep fixed costs and variable costs distinct

Variable costs change with production or sales, while fixed costs remain within a relevant operating range. The distinction is useful but sometimes requires judgment. A monthly software subscription may be fixed at one scale and increase when your team or order volume changes. Equipment maintenance can also be uneven across months.

The U.S. Small Business Administration explains break-even analysis in terms of fixed costs, selling price and variable cost per unit. For a single-product illustration, fixed costs divided by the contribution per unit gives a break-even quantity. This is a simplified model, not a forecast. A multi-product business, changing fees or limited production capacity can make the calculation more complicated. Label the assumptions instead of presenting a calculated quantity as guaranteed demand.

## Model returns, discounts and advertising separately

An order that ships is not always an order that stays sold. Set aside a place to record returned items, replacements and refunds. Use your own observed history when it becomes available. If you are starting from an estimate, make that uncertainty visible and test more than one scenario.

Advertising also deserves its own line. A campaign's sales figure does not tell you how much remains after product and channel costs. Compare spending with the contribution from the relevant orders, while recognizing that attribution is imperfect. For an early worksheet, it is enough to compare a no-advertising scenario with one or two clearly labeled spending scenarios. Avoid blending every cost into a single percentage that cannot be checked against an invoice or transaction report.

## Build a worksheet another person can follow

Use columns for the component, cost per unit, evidence, date checked and notes. Then add a summary with selling price, variable cost, contribution and separately listed fixed costs. Keep assumptions beside the calculation. A figure without an explanation becomes difficult to maintain when packaging prices or platform rules change.

- Define exactly what is included in one sellable unit.
- Record material use from completed production, including ordinary losses.
- List packing and fulfillment work as well as manufacturing work.
- Check current channel deductions against an actual settlement.
- Separate fixed costs, returns and advertising assumptions.
- Revisit the sheet when the product, process or sales channel changes.

You can use a simple version for each variation and a separate overview for the whole business. The important feature is traceability. Someone reviewing the sheet should be able to see which figures come from receipts, which come from observations and which are still estimates. Keep customer personal data out of a general costing file unless it is necessary and protected appropriately.

## Next steps: test the result against real orders

After a small batch of sales, compare expected and actual materials, deductions, packing time and returns. Investigate meaningful differences before expanding production. A lower-than-expected remainder does not automatically mean the price must rise; it might point to waste, an unsuitable bundle or a channel whose costs do not fit the product.
